Abstract

RoadWatch is a proposed AI-assisted pothole detection and repair-prioritisation platform intended exclusively for Mumbai roads. This Semester III investigation establishes its detection foundation through a controlled pilot experiment and an interactive prototype. A public road-image dataset was converted to a one-class YOLO detection format and audited using SHA-256 hashing. Of 819 source images, 84 duplicate copies were removed, resulting in 735 images and 1,906 pothole annotations. Two COCO-pretrained YOLOv8 variants, nano and small, were fine-tuned under identical documented settings: five epochs, 416 × 416 input resolution, batch size eight, CPU execution and seed 42.

YOLOv8n was selected using validation mAP@0.5 (0.6268 versus 0.5837). On the 79-image held-out test split containing 203 labelled potholes, YOLOv8n achieved precision 0.6377, recall 0.4335, F1 0.5161, mAP@0.5 0.5367 and throughput 10.41 images/s. YOLOv8s yielded precision 0.5444, recall 0.4532, F1 0.4946, mAP@0.5 0.4945 and 5.75 images/s. Both detectors missed more than half the test annotations at the fixed operating threshold, demonstrating the need for further optimisation. A Streamlit prototype supports image inference and comparative analysis. Faster R-CNN and SSD are reviewed but were not trained.

The current data are not verified as Mumbai-specific; therefore, the findings establish a public-data baseline rather than local effectiveness. Future research will use independently collected Mumbai imagery, evaluate robustness across road conditions, and develop location-based duplicate handling and human-validated severity and priority scoring. The system is not yet ready for operational municipal use.

Keywords: Pothole detection; Mumbai; YOLOv8; transfer learning; data integrity; object detection; repair prioritisation; computer vision.



Chapter 1: Introduction & Problem Formulation

1.1 Background & Mumbai Motivation

Potholes are localised depressions in pavement that can affect road usability and vehicle safety. Mumbai is the sole intended geographical domain of RoadWatch. Monsoon rain, water accumulation, shadows, variable pavement appearance and dense traffic motivate a detector that is evaluated on local conditions rather than assumed to generalise from a public dataset. Image-based reporting offers a practical input mechanism, but an image alone does not establish physical depth or the urgency of a repair.

Computer vision can locate damage in photographs and provide bounding boxes that guide human review. Deep-learning methods learn visual patterns from labelled examples instead of relying only on hand-coded rules. RoadWatch uses this capability as the first step of an intended reporting workflow.

1.2 Problem Statement

A lightweight detector must identify visible potholes with useful recall and manageable false alarms under resource constraints. For Mumbai, a further challenge is whether a model trained on publicly available imagery generalises to local streets. Repeated reports may refer to the same physical pothole, while image perspective and water can obscure severity. The present study therefore tests two compact detectors on a common public benchmark before undertaking local adaptation and repair-ranking research.

1.3 Significance and Scope of the Work

•  Current scope: single-class image-based pothole detection, exact-duplicate audit, matched CPU experiment, error analysis and Streamlit demonstration.

•  Future geographic scope: Mumbai municipal road locations only. Images from other regions may serve as pretraining sources, not evidence of Mumbai performance.

•  Excluded: crack classification, other cities, autonomous repair decisions, certified depth measurement and live municipal deployment.



Chapter 2: Literature Survey & Gap Analysis

2.1 Literature Survey: Detailed Review of Prior Work

Redmon et al. [1] introduced YOLO as a unified object-detection formulation that predicts classes and object locations directly. Its one-stage design motivates rapid inference, but the original YOLO paper is not a description of YOLOv8. Ren et al. [2] presented Faster R-CNN, in which a region proposal network generates candidate regions for further classification and refinement. Liu et al. [3] introduced SSD, a one-stage detector that makes predictions at multiple feature scales. These works establish alternative detector families; their original benchmark results cannot be compared directly with RoadWatch because the datasets and experimental conditions differ.

Ultralytics [4] documents YOLOv8, including its anchor-free detection head and model-size variants. RoadWatch uses pretrained nano and small detection checkpoints for transfer learning. The choice of a compact detector reflects the CPU-only pilot constraint rather than a claim of global superiority.

Maeda et al. [7] investigated road-damage detection using smartphone imagery and labelled damage instances, illustrating the feasibility of camera-based road inspection. The RDD2022 dataset paper by Arya et al. [6] provides 47,420 road images from six countries, including India, and covers four damage categories. For RoadWatch, RDD2022 is a possible source for pothole-only transfer learning, but Indian imagery must not be equated with confirmed Mumbai observations.

A review of road imaging and pothole detection [8] discusses classical two-dimensional processing, 3-D point-cloud approaches, machine learning and hybrid systems. A later review [9] also identifies variations in road appearance and imaging conditions as challenges for reliable detection. These studies motivate condition-wise testing and caution against treating a two-dimensional bounding box as a physical severity measurement.

The literature supports the technical direction but does not establish that RoadWatch is the first system to combine detection and mapping. Its proposed contribution is a locally evaluated, explainable workflow for Mumbai, supported by transparent experiments and human verification.

The preprocessing pipeline merges “pothole” and “potholes” into one category. Six generic “object” annotations were ignored rather than assumed to be potholes. Bounding boxes were clipped to image boundaries; no invalid boxes were skipped. SHA-256 hashing identified 84 exact-duplicate groups in total, including 40 groups crossing split boundaries. Keeping test copies before validation and training copies removed 84 repeated images and corresponding labels. The 84 groups and 84 removed images are different measures, as reported in the project brief.

Bounding-Box Conversion

For a clipped COCO box [x, y, w, h] in an image with width W and height H, the YOLO label is class_id followed by the normalised centre coordinates and box dimensions.

Integrity and limitations

Exact hashing prevents byte-identical images from appearing in more than one cleaned partition. It does not detect different encodings, resized versions, consecutive frames or photographs of the same physical pothole. A later perceptual-hash and location/route audit is needed. Only nine clean images contain no target annotation; this does not prove the absence of unlabelled damage and provides limited negative-scene coverage.

Model architecture

YOLOv8 is a convolutional object detector. Its backbone extracts spatial features, the neck combines information across scales, and its anchor-free head predicts object locations and class scores [4]. Nano and small use related architectures at different capacities. Transfer learning starts from pretrained weights rather than random initialisation and adapts them to the pothole class.

3.5 Evaluation Metrics & Mathematical Framework

Predictions at confidence ≥ 0.25 are sorted by confidence and matched greedily to unmatched ground-truth boxes with IoU ≥ 0.50. A match is a true positive (TP); an unmatched prediction is a false positive (FP); an unmatched labelled pothole is a false negative (FN). NMS at IoU 0.70 is used for inference suppression and is distinct from the matching threshold.

IoU = Area(prediction ∩ ground truth) / Area(prediction ∪ ground truth)

Eq. (3) Intersection over Union.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Eq. (4) Fraction of predicted detections that match labels.

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

Eq. (5) Fraction of labelled potholes matched by predictions.

F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall)

Eq. (6) Harmonic mean of precision and recall.

mAP@0.5:0.95 = mean of AP at IoU thresholds 0.50, 0.55, …, 0.95

Eq. (7) Multi-threshold average precision.

Average precision (AP) summarises the precision–recall curve at a given matching IoU; with one class, mAP equals AP for that class. mAP@0.5 uses IoU 0.50. Unlike fixed-threshold P/R/F1, these AP metrics aggregate ranked predictions over confidence operating points. Validation P/R in this study are Ultralytics curve-summary values, so they are not directly comparable to fixed-threshold test P/R.

Throughput = Number of processed images / elapsed inference time (s)

Eq. (8) Images per second; hardware- and implementation-specific.

Chapter 5: Conclusion & Future Directions

5.1 Conclusion and Limitations

The Semester III study establishes a reproducible one-class detection baseline using 735 cleaned images and 1,906 labelled potholes. The exact-duplicate audit addresses a practical risk of data leakage. Under the documented short CPU experiment, YOLOv8n achieves the stronger validation mAP@0.5 and is selected for the interactive prototype. It reaches 0.5367 test mAP@0.5 and 10.41 images/s, but its recall of 0.4335 highlights a substantial detection limitation.

The completed contribution is a matched pilot comparison, documented data preparation and an inference demonstration. The proposed contribution is a locally evaluated Mumbai pothole reporting and priority workflow; novelty and usefulness must be demonstrated in Phase II rather than asserted in advance.

Abstract

Pothole detection using computer vision can assist road inspection by transforming citizen photographs into candidate damage reports. RoadWatch is a proposed Mumbai-specific pothole detection, severity assessment and repair-prioritisation system. This study establishes the detector-stage baseline through a controlled comparison of two COCO-pretrained YOLOv8 configurations. A public dataset of 819 road images was cleaned using annotation standardisation and SHA-256 exact-image deduplication, yielding 735 images and 1,906 pothole bounding boxes. Both detectors were fine-tuned for five epochs on CPU using identical data partitions, input resolution and batch size. YOLOv8n was selected by validation mAP@0.5 (0.6268 versus 0.5837). On 79 held-out images with 203 labelled potholes, YOLOv8n achieved precision 0.6377, recall 0.4335 (rounded), F1 0.5161 and mAP@0.5 0.5367 at 10.41 images/s. YOLOv8s achieved precision 0.5444, recall 0.4532, F1 0.4946 and mAP@0.5 0.4945 at 5.75 images/s. Both detectors missed over half of the labelled potholes at the fixed operating threshold. An interactive Streamlit prototype supports image inference and inspection of these results. The findings establish a preliminary baseline, not a validated Mumbai deployment; local data collection, recall improvement, geographic deduplication and human-validated severity assessment are proposed for subsequent research.

Keywords: Pothole detection, road damage, YOLOv8, computer vision, data leakage, transfer learning, Mumbai, repair prioritisation.

Introduction

Potholes are a practical road-maintenance concern because road-surface damage may disrupt travel and create hazards. RoadWatch focuses exclusively on the municipal context of Mumbai, where an image-based reporting workflow could support human inspection and prioritisation. The present investigation addresses the first technical dependency: locating potholes in road photographs with a compact object detector.



A pothole detection system differs from a conventional image classifier: a classifier provides an image-level category, whereas a detector must identify the position of each visible damage instance. A citizen photograph can contain several potholes, shadows, markings or unrelated objects, so detection requires both recognition and localisation. Conditions such as low light, wet surfaces, water-filled depressions and camera viewpoint can complicate visual interpretation. These conditions motivate Mumbai-specific testing but were not annotated in the current public dataset.

An operational reporting system also requires more than a detector. Different photographs of the same physical pothole may create duplicate reports, while the apparent size of a pothole changes with camera distance and perspective. A high-confidence detection therefore does not establish physical severity or repair urgency. The RoadWatch architecture separates visual evidence from geographic association, damage assessment and municipal decision support. Only the first stage has been evaluated in this semester.

The research question is whether a compact YOLOv8n configuration or a higher-capacity YOLOv8s configuration produces a stronger validation detection score when trained with the same short CPU protocol, and what trade-off is observed in held-out recall, false alarms and processing throughput. The experiment does not claim to identify the best detector across all architectures or establish generalisation to Mumbai roads.

For a predicted bounding box Bp and a ground-truth box Bg, localisation agreement is quantified using Intersection over Union (IoU). A prediction is eligible for a correct match only when the overlap satisfies the chosen criterion:

IoU(Bp, Bg) = Area(Bp ∩ Bg) / Area(Bp ∪ Bg)

(1) Intersection over Union for bounding-box overlap.

Literature Review

Redmon et al. [1] introduced the YOLO formulation, in which detection is performed through a unified network rather than a separate region-proposal stage. The one-stage approach provides a useful foundation for applications in which processing time matters. RoadWatch uses the later YOLOv8 implementation documented by Ultralytics [4], not the original YOLO architecture. The Nano and Small variants allow model capacity to be compared without changing the underlying detector family.

Ren et al. [2] proposed Faster R-CNN, which uses a Region Proposal Network followed by region classification and box refinement. Liu et al. [3] proposed SSD, a one-stage detector predicting boxes across multiple feature-map scales. These methods provide relevant architectural alternatives. They are reviewed as literature baselines only: neither was trained or assigned experimental scores in the present work.



Road-damage-specific research provides a closer context than general-purpose object detection. Maeda et al. [7] investigated the detection and classification of road damage from smartphone images, motivating accessible image capture as an alternative source of inspection evidence. Arya et al. [6] introduced RDD2022, a multi-national road-damage dataset that includes Indian imagery. Its availability motivates an India-oriented transfer-learning study, but an Indian subset cannot automatically be treated as a verified Mumbai test set.

Reviews of computer vision for road imaging and pothole detection [8], [9] provide broader context for the limitations of visual detection and the range of existing approaches. They motivate the distinction between detecting visible damage and estimating its physical geometry. The present study does not compare numerical results reported by unrelated papers because differences in datasets, annotation policies, image sizes and hardware would make a direct ranking misleading.

The project addresses four specific research needs: (1) auditing exact-image duplicates across partitions before training; (2) comparing two lightweight detector scales under a matched protocol; (3) reporting missed detections and false alarms alongside aggregate AP and throughput; and (4) establishing a pathway toward an independently evaluated Mumbai-only reporting workflow. The first three are implemented in the pilot, while the fourth remains a proposed research direction. These are project-specific contributions, not claims that the underlying techniques are novel.

METHODOLOGY

The pilot uses a publicly accessible Road Damage Dataset originally attributed to abdulmohsen-fahad on Roboflow Universe and mirrored as manot/pothole-segmentation on Hugging Face [5]. Source labels “pothole” and “potholes” are unified; six generic “object” annotations are excluded rather than assumed to be potholes.

The cleaned dataset contains nine images without retained pothole annotations (four training, three validation and two test). The test partition is held apart from model-configuration selection. Its small size and unknown geographic composition limit the interpretation of the results.



COCO annotations describe a bounding box using its top-left position and width and height. After clipping coordinates to the image boundaries, the preprocessing pipeline converts boxes to YOLO normalised centre coordinates. For a clipped box (x, y, w, h) in an image of width W and height H, the transformation is:

cx = (x + w/2)/W;  cy = (y + h/2)/H;  bw = w/W;  bh = h/H

(2) COCO-to-YOLO bounding-box normalisation.

Each annotation is written with class identifier 0 and the four normalised values. No invalid boxes were skipped in the documented run. The same cleaned files are used for both detectors. This preserves a common evaluation basis and avoids silently relabelling ambiguous generic annotations.

SHA-256 hashing is applied to the image bytes before training. The audit identifies 84 exact-duplicate groups, including 40 groups crossing split boundaries. For duplicate images, the test copy is retained first, followed by validation and training; lower-priority copies and their labels are removed. A total of 84 duplicate images were removed. This addresses byte-identical leakage, but does not detect near-duplicate images produced by compression, resizing, cropping or sequential capture.

3.2 Transfer Learning and Controlled YOLOv8 Experimental Design

Both configurations begin with COCO-pretrained weights, permitting the models to reuse learned visual features rather than training all parameters from random initialisation. YOLOv8n is the smaller model and YOLOv8s provides more capacity. The model family uses a convolutional feature extractor, multi-scale feature aggregation and a detection head to predict object locations and confidence. The study compares the provided Ultralytics implementations rather than proposing a new neural architecture.

3.3 Object Detection Techniques and Performance Evaluation

The validation split is used to select the preferred model configuration. The documented rule selects the model with the highest validation mAP@0.5, leaving the test data for final reporting. To evaluate errors at a specific operating point, predictions are filtered at confidence 0.25 and matched in descending confidence order to at most one unmatched ground-truth box with IoU at least 0.50. A matched box is a true positive (TP), an unmatched prediction is a false positive (FP), and an unmatched labelled pothole is a false negative (FN). Non-maximum suppression (NMS) removes redundant overlapping predictions before this matching step.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP);     Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

(3) Precision and recall at a fixed operating threshold.

F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall)

(4) Harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Average precision (AP) summarises the ranked precision–recall curve. Because the dataset has one class, mean AP equals the AP of the pothole class. mAP@0.5 measures detection performance at an IoU of 0.50; mAP@0.5:0.95 averages AP over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95. These are distinct from P/R/F1 calculated using the single confidence threshold of 0.25.

mAP@0.5:0.95 = (1/10) Σ AP(0.50 + 0.05k),  k = 0,…,9

(5) AP averaged over progressively stricter overlap criteria.

Throughput = Number of evaluated images / Elapsed inference time (seconds)

(6) Measured CPU image processing throughput.

Validation precision and recall are Ultralytics curve-summary metrics; test precision and recall are obtained from fixed-threshold matching. They are not directly comparable as a before-and-after pair. Speed is reported on the local CPU under the documented inference measurement, not as a general hardware-normalised benchmark.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This study establishes a documented baseline for RoadWatch through a shared, cleaned public dataset, matched YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s experiments, explicit validation-based selection and fixed-threshold error analysis. YOLOv8n is the preferred pilot configuration, with validation mAP@0.5 of 0.6268, test mAP@0.5 of 0.5367 and measured CPU throughput of 10.41 images/s. Its limited recall is the principal research problem identified by the experiment. The Streamlit prototype demonstrates the detector stage; geographic reporting and repair prioritisation have not yet been validated.

The next phase will restrict the final application and local evaluation to Mumbai. Safely collected road photographs will be labelled by pothole instance, road segment, lighting and weather, with negative examples and explicit treatment of water-filled or ambiguous damage. Route- or segment-disjoint partitions and near-duplicate checks will reduce leakage. RDD2022 may be used for additional transfer learning, but a separate Mumbai test set will remain untouched during optimisation. Longer GPU training, multiple random seeds, validation-only confidence selection and a cross-family comparison will examine recall improvement without unacceptable false alarms. Results will be reported by condition and with uncertainty where supported.

The planned reporting pipeline will associate user-confirmed coordinates and time with candidate potholes, merge geographically and visually similar reports, and rank verified damage using explainable factors. A proposed priority function combines independently assessed severity, road exposure and report age using non-negative weights summing to one. Confidence indicates detection uncertainty, not depth or danger. Human assessment and, where feasible, measured physical dimensions will be required to validate severity. A municipal dashboard will present Mumbai-only candidate reports for human review and repair tracking. These modules remain proposed work and no performance or municipal adoption claims are made.

78. No flagged match · Not checked · 0%
